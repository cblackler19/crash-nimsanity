# Asset investigation notes

These notes record observations from the local NTSC-U/C v1.00 disc image. The image and executable remain local research inputs and are not part of the repository.

## ISO layout

The ISO9660 volume label is `TWINSANITY`, with a 2048-byte logical block size. The game files are under `CRASH6`:

| File | ISO LBA | Length in bytes |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| `AMERICAN.MB` | 469279 | 13,121,536 |
| `AMERICAN.MH` | 475686 | 2,868 |
| `CRASH.BD` | 475688 | 890,464,828 |
| `CRASH.BH` | 910486 | 27,376 |
| `MUSIC.MB` | 910500 | 221,773,824 |
| `MUSIC.MH` | 1018788 | 2,868 |

The listed extents are consecutive after rounding each file up to ISO logical blocks. This describes disc placement; it does not establish how the game relates or loads the similarly named files.

## `.MH` observations

Both `.MH` files have the same length and begin with these 8 bytes:

```text
8F 00 00 00 00 00 01 00
```

Interpreting the first two words as little-endian integers gives `143` and `65536`. The remaining 2,860 bytes divide evenly into 143 records of 20 bytes each. Each record can therefore be viewed as five little-endian 32-bit words for inspection; their meanings are not yet known.

Treating word 1 as a candidate byte length and word 2 as a candidate byte offset keeps all 143 ranges inside each corresponding `.MB` file. In record zero, those words are `309024` and `12812288` for `AMERICAN.MH`, and `309024` and `221464576` for `MUSIC.MH`; each range ends 224 bytes before the end of its `.MB` file.

All 143 `AMERICAN.MH` candidate offsets begin an `MSVp` byte sequence in `AMERICAN.MB`. The 13 records in `MUSIC.MH` with word 0 equal to `0` also begin `MSVp` sequences in `MUSIC.MB`. At these range starts, the big-endian word at byte 4 is `32`, and the big-endian word at byte 12 plus 48 equals the candidate range length for every checked entry. This links those records to bounded `MSVp` ranges; it does not identify the format's fields.

The ISC-licensed vgmstream `msv.c` reader identifies `MSVp` as Sony MultiStream and interprets big-endian `+0x0C` as channel data length, `+0x10` as sample rate, and `+0x30` as the PSX ADPCM data start; its reader treats the stream as mono. Applying those field locations to the local `MUSIC.MB` records, all 13 type-0 entries have a 48-byte prefix followed by the declared channel-data length, and all have sample-rate field `32000`. The string at `+0x20` is `L05_Chickens` for record 139, `B01_MechEndfall` for record 140, and `undefined` for the other sampled range. The 13 records reference only three distinct offset/length ranges: eleven entries alias one 309,024-byte range, and records 139 and 140 each reference their own range. This supports interpreting the entries as stream references; no audio bytes were decoded here, and the meaning of header word `+4` remains open.

The 62 `MUSIC.MH` records with word 0 equal to `1` point to ranges that begin without `MSVp`. Sorted by offset, these ranges are contiguous from byte zero through byte `221118464`. The remaining 68 records have word 0 equal to `2` and words 1 through 4 all zero. These groupings are observed values; their meanings remain unknown.

At the `MSVp` range starts, the big-endian word at byte 16 matches `.MH` word 3 for all 13 type-0 music entries and 140 of 143 American entries. The exceptions are American records 125, 131, and 134: their `.MH` word 3 is `32000`, while the corresponding `.MB` word is `18000`, `48000`, and `48000`. Word 3 therefore should not be treated as a universal copy of that `.MB` field.

In `MUSIC.MH`, records 7 through 10 have word 0 equal to `1`. Their word 2 values are `0`, `3932160`, `8912896`, and `13631488`; for each record after the first, word 2 equals the prior record's word 2 plus its word 1. This is consistent with a cumulative range or offset field across those four records, though the address space and meaning are still unknown.

All 62 type-1 `MUSIC.MH` ranges begin with 16 zero bytes. In the first 228 bytes, 26 ranges have little-endian `0x000C` at every 16-byte step from offset `0x10` through `0xD0`; only record 7 has little-endian `0x44` at offset `0xE0`. Scanning all 13,819,904 aligned 16-byte blocks in those ranges found that every first byte has a high nibble no greater than 4 and every second byte is `0x00`. This is a strong match for the PSX ADPCM block header shape: FFmpeg's decoder reads the high nibble of byte 0 as the filter and byte 1 as the block flag, then decodes 28 samples per block. The all-zero second bytes indicate ordinary non-terminal blocks under that decoder's flag handling. Every range length is divisible by 32, so each contains an integral number of pairs of 16-byte blocks. The raw type-1 `.MH` word at +12 is `32000` in 54 records and `48000` in 8. Since the same word matches the `MSVp` sample-rate field for all 13 type-0 music records, these values support (but do not prove) interpreting it as the type-1 sample rate too. The raw word at +16 is divisible by 16 in all 62 records, with 55 distinct values ranging from 688 to 64,912; its meaning remains unknown. `mh_inspect` reads the payload directly from the ISO and reports header distributions and raw record words.

The user identified the original mono decode of type-1 record 7 as the Bandicoot Pursuit soundtrack, but reported repeated sections. A first stereo hypothesis that alternated every 16-byte block produced a WAV the user described as roughly twice as fast, with garbled repeats. This listening result rejects that block-by-block channel assignment; the earlier runtime match was not enough to support it. The public soundtrack lists “Twisted Docamok” (Bandicoot Pursuit) at 1:48 ([Amazon Music track listing](https://music.amazon.com/tracks/B0F3Y5R1VB)), but that duration alone does not determine the in-game file's playback rate or loop behavior.

One unrelated PS2 audio investigation reports stereo channel interleaving in 0x800-byte chunks and warns that splitting at every 16-byte ADPCM block can yield approximately twice-speed audio ([MGS2 format investigation](https://reshax.com/topic/19287-reverse-engineering-metal-gear-solid-2-audio-formats-sdt-sdx/)). That is only a lead from another game and format, not evidence for Twinsanity. All 62 type-1 ranges can be divided into paired 0x800-byte chunks. The user listened to record 7 candidates made with that chunk size at 32 kHz and 16 kHz and rejected both: the 32 kHz version sounds roughly 1.5 times too fast, the 16 kHz version roughly half-speed, and both retain repetitions while musical parts alternate between the left and right ears. Thus the 0x800-byte stereo split, its assumed sample-rate handling, or both are wrong; the matching nominal runtime was misleading.

`mh_audio_export` exposes the channel chunk size (in 16-byte ADPCM blocks) and optional sample-rate override to compare hypotheses without changing the ISO reader. Its default of 128 blocks (0x800 bytes) per channel chunk is now a rejected candidate, not a recommended setting. It resets each channel's predictor history at the record boundary and reads the payload directly from the ISO. FFmpeg's PSX ADPCM decoder provided a formula reference for independent implementation; no FFmpeg code was copied. Export with `nim c --path:src --out:tools/mh_audio_export tools/mh_audio_export.nim` followed by `tools/mh_audio_export <image.iso> 7 <output.wav> [channel-chunk-blocks] [sample-rate]`. The next investigation should follow the executable's music-stream setup and playback parameters, then validate the channel/loop interpretation against that code.

## `.BH` / `.BD` and executable clues

`CRASH.BH` begins with the little-endian word `1281` (`0x501`). After that word, the file parses exactly into 612 variable-length entries. Each entry contains a little-endian path byte length, that many path bytes, a little-endian data offset, and a little-endian data length. The entries consume the index to EOF. In table order, every data offset equals the prior range's end, starting at byte zero, and the final range ends at byte `890464828`, exactly the length of `CRASH.BD`.

The independent `BDArchive.cs` reader and writer in Twinsanity Editor call the leading word a magic value and write `0x501` when rebuilding an archive. Its reader likewise consumes each name-length/name/offset/size record and seeks to that offset in the paired `.BD`. This corroborates the layout found above and identifies `0x501` as the editor's archive header value; the interpretation is cross-checked against this project's own ISO data rather than copied code.

The read-only Nim utility [`bh_inspect.nim`](../tools/bh_inspect.nim) parses this layout, reports each path and range, and can check that ranges stay within a supplied `.BD` file. The data image itself was not extracted. `CRASH.BD` begins with `03 00 00 00`; this alone does not identify its internal format.

The archive index contains 135 `.RM2` resources and 134 `.SM2` resources. Matching the paths by directory and base name gives 134 pairs; `Startup\Default.rm2` is the unmatched `.RM2` entry. The MIT-licensed Twinsanity Editor describes an RM/SM pair as a complete level chunk, providing a useful lead for inspecting these resources.

The editor's `TwinsFile.cs` describes a 12-byte file header followed by a count of 12-byte section records containing offset, size, and ID, then section payloads. Three local ISO samples agree: `Levels\Earth\Cavern\cavent.rm2` and `.sm2`, and `Startup\Default.rm2`, each begin with word `0x00010001`; their section counts are 12, 7, and 12. For all three, the header's content-size word equals file length minus the header and section table, and the section ranges are contiguous from the end of the table through EOF. The section directory structure is corroborated; payload meanings still need direct validation.

The read-only Nim utility [`rm2_inspect.nim`](../tools/rm2_inspect.nim) locates an RM2 or SM2 path in the on-ISO `.BH` catalog, reads its header and section table directly from the `.BD` extent, and reports bounded section ranges. It does not extract resource payloads.

The editor maps RM2 section ID 11 and SM2 section ID 6 to graphics sections. The nested sections in both `cavent` graphics samples use the same `0x00010001` header and contain IDs 0 through 8. `TwinsSection.cs` maps those child IDs to texture, material, model, rigid model, skin, blend skin, mesh, LOD model, and skydome records. In the local samples, child ID 2 is 2,000,804 bytes in RM2 and 424,404 bytes in SM2; child ID 0 is 461,132 bytes in RM2 and 351,516 bytes in SM2. These names are the editor's classifications; the payload layouts and render semantics still need direct inspection.

The editor's `Model.Load` reader describes each ID-2 model item as a submodel count followed by records containing a vertex count, a VIF byte length and payload, and a blob byte length and payload. The on-ISO reader now walks that structure with per-item bounds checks, without interpreting the VIF instructions. In `cavent.rm2`, all 66 model items parse to the end of their declared ranges with no blob or trailing bytes; they contain 86 submodels, 45,983 declared vertices, and 1,998,704 VIF bytes. In `cavent.sm2`, all 13 model items likewise parse exactly, containing 44 submodels, 14,220 declared vertices, and 423,656 VIF bytes. These measurements confirm the editor's record boundaries for these two samples; they do not confirm the meaning of every VIF operation or vertex attribute.

The Nim model decoder now reads VIF UNPACK payloads for the observed address-3 V3_32 positions and address-4 V4_32 UV/color records, checks that both streams match each submodel's declared vertex count, and expands the editor-classified connection markers into alternating-winding strip triangles. Every model item in the two Cavern samples decodes: 86 RM2 submodels produce 26,939 candidate triangles, and 44 SM2 submodels produce 9,838. The preview follows the model's rigid-model link to a material and uses its first shader texture that resolves in the sibling texture table. For RM2 model `4263539267`, this selects material `1222581543` and texture `3462493108` (64×32). This follows the editor's attribute and triangle interpretation, including its use of low VIF address bits; it does not resolve FLG/TOPS relocation or reproduce every material shader setting.

The editor's `Texture.Load` reader describes texture items as a 32-bit size followed by a fixed 224-byte metadata area and the image payload. It reads width and height exponents, a pixel-format code, mip count, texture-buffer fields, and the transfer rectangle dimensions from this header. The on-ISO child-ID-0 reader checks these records with bounds validation. All 44 Cavern RM2 textures and all 31 paired SM2 textures have an item length exactly four bytes greater than the stored texture size, valid dimension exponents from 0 through 10, and pixel-format code `0x13` (PSMT8, as classified by the editor). Every image payload length equals the corresponding transfer rectangle's width times height times four bytes. The texture IDs are preserved in the item table and resolve against shader references in the sibling material table.

The editor's texture path stages those bytes into a simulated GS buffer using the recorded transfer rectangle and PSMCT32 layout. It then reads the base image using PSMT8 addressing and the texture-buffer width, reads a 16×16 PSMCT32 CLUT from the recorded CLUT base pointer, rearranges the palette entries, and maps each indexed texel through that palette. The editor's `EzSwizzle` implementation describes PSMT8 pages as 128×64 pixels, split into 16×16 blocks and smaller columns; the PS2SDK independently defines format code `0x13` as PSMT8. The Nim decoder adapts this page/block/column mapping and palette path in memory. It successfully decodes all 44 RM2 and 31 SM2 Cavern textures (302,336 base-image pixels and 181 mip levels totaling 98,816 pixels) and follows the recorded TBP/TBW pointers; one 128×128 RM2 base texture was visually reviewed as a coherent rocky surface. The renderer can now locate a selected texture through the ISO archive and display it on a preview quad, including the stored mip levels. This confirms the mapping, CLUT path, and mip pointer ranges for these samples, but not every material's rendering settings.

The editor's `DMATag` reader takes the low 16 bits of the first word as QWC, and its VIF interpreter reads that many 16-byte units after the 16-byte tag. Applying that length check to the local VIF streams yields no overrun in either sample: 22 of 86 RM2 streams and 10 of 44 SM2 streams match `16 + QWC * 16` exactly. The rest have 12–36 bytes after that range, and every such byte is zero. For the first `cavent.rm2` stream, the QWC candidate is 973, giving 15,584 tag-plus-payload bytes inside a 15,620-byte VIF field, with 36 zero bytes following. The residual bytes are observed; their purpose is not yet established. This length check alone does not decode VIF payloads or verify the PS2's full DMA behavior.

The inspector now walks VIF command boundaries inside the tag's 8-byte command area plus its QWC-bounded payload. It accounts for command words, UNPACK payload sizes (including STCYCL cycle lengths and the packed V4-5 form), STMASK, STROW/STCOL, MPG, and DIRECT/DIRECTHL payloads. The scan uses little-endian command words and checks each payload against the bounded DMA span. Across the Cavern samples it walks 13,308 commands in 86 RM2 streams and 3,550 commands in 44 SM2 streams. It finds 7,700 and 1,700 UNPACK commands respectively; all streams finish on a command boundary, with no unsupported opcode or truncated payload encountered. This validates command boundaries for these samples only; the inspector does not reconstruct VU memory, decode vertex attributes, or establish triangle connectivity.

For each submodel, the inspector also sums each UNPACK command's NUM vector count by its 10-bit immediate address and compares those totals with the submodel's declared vertex count. The editor's `Model.CalculateData` classifies addresses 3, 4, 5, and 6 as vertex, UV/color, normal, and emissive-color outputs. In the Cavern RM2, addresses 3, 4, and 6 match the declared count in all 86 streams; address 5 matches in all 80 streams that use it. In the paired SM2, addresses 3 and 4 match in all 44 streams. The UNPACK formats at those addresses are consistent across each sample: RM2 address 3 uses V3_32 (`0x68`), address 4 uses V4_32 (`0x6C`), address 5 uses V3_32 (`0x68`), and address 6 uses V4_8 (`0x6E`); SM2 addresses 3 and 4 use V3_32 and V4_32 respectively. Together, the address counts, formats, and editor classifications strongly support these being per-vertex attribute streams. The check uses the immediate address field only; it does not resolve BASE/OFFSET/FLG addressing or interpret the packed values.

As a first value-level check, the inspector views address-3 V3_32 words as little-endian binary32 coordinates. This produces exactly the declared number of finite position vectors in both samples: 45,983 in the Cavern RM2 and 14,220 in the SM2. The combined RM2 sample ranges are X -2.720..2.885, Y -2.637..3.082, Z -2.718..2.756; the SM2 ranges are approximately -120.487..120.487 on each axis. These bounded values are consistent with local model geometry, but the combined bounds do not reveal world placement, coordinate conversion, or vertex connectivity.

Address-4 V4_32 words also yield finite UV candidates when the low byte of each of the first two words is cleared and V is flipped as in the editor reader. Across the Cavern RM2 sample, UV ranges are U -2.726..4.000 and V -2.127..10.451; the SM2 ranges are U -6.299..7.970 and V -2.500..5.998. Values outside 0..1 are observed, so the coordinates may use tiling or another convention; texture interpretation is not established. The same packed words contain low-byte color candidates and a connection flag in the W word, matching the editor's field extraction. Every one of the 130 submodel streams starts with flags `false, false, true`, and position and connection-vector counts align in all 130 streams. The editor's PLY exporter counts faces by testing `Conn` on the third vertex, although its face-writing loop tests it on the first; that internal mismatch suggests an unintentional offset in one path. Interpreting `Conn` on the third vertex as the face marker yields 26,939 candidate RM2 triangles, of which 26,937 have nonzero area and two are near-degenerate, plus 9,838 nonzero-area SM2 triangles. This strongly supports triangle-strip-like connectivity, but the triangle order and winding still need visual confirmation.

Address-5 V3_32 normal candidates are finite and nearly unit length in the RM2 sample: 36,660 vectors have lengths from 0.9999854 to 1.0000018, and all 80 streams containing address 5 match their declared vertex counts. The remaining six RM2 streams have no address-5 UNPACK vectors. Address 6 decodes as V4_8 emissive-byte candidates for all 45,983 RM2 vertices, with observed channel ranges from `[0, 0, 0, 0]` to `[255, 255, 255, 119]`. The normal magnitudes support the editor's normal classification; emissive channel meaning and color-space behavior remain unverified. The paired SM2 sample has no address-5 or address-6 streams.

`rm2_inspect` accepts an optional outer section index to inspect that section's nested table, and an optional nested section index to parse the model item records. For example, `tools/rm2_inspect.exe "gamefiles/Crash Twinsanity (v1.00).iso" Levels\Earth\Cavern\cavent.rm2 0 2` selects outer table position 0 (ID 11) and nested table position 2 (ID 2). This reads the selected resource's section table and model records directly from the ISO; it does not extract payload files.

The editor maps graphics child ID 3 to rigid models and child ID 6 to meshes; both are read through its `RigidModel` parser, which expects a header word, a material-ID count and list, and a mesh ID. The on-ISO records in `cavent.rm2` (child ID 3) and `cavent.sm2` (child ID 6) fit that layout exactly: 66 RM2 items with header `0x101` and 86 material references, and 13 SM2 items with header `0x104` and 44 material references. Each has one distinct mesh reference per record. The inspector now checks those references against sibling child ID 2 model item IDs: all 66 RM2 references and all 13 SM2 references resolve. This supports a relationship between the field and model records in these samples, but does not establish how the game uses the relationship or what the differing header values mean.

The editor's `Material.Load` reader describes material records as an eight-byte header, an unknown word, a length-prefixed name, a shader count, and variable-size shader records. The bounded reader parses the Cavern material section using those type-dependent shader sizes and compares each shader's texture ID with the sibling texture table. In `cavent.rm2`, all 70 material records end at their declared boundaries, containing 71 shader records whose texture IDs all resolve among the 44 textures. All 86 material links in the 66 sibling rigid models also resolve to those material IDs, while all 66 mesh links resolve to model IDs. In `cavent.sm2`, all 41 material records parse exactly and their 41 shader texture IDs resolve among its 31 textures. These checks establish a consistent record-reference chain for the sample tables, not the rendering behavior of each shader setting.

The model inspector follows the linked rigid-model record and reports every distinct material/texture pair whose shader texture ID resolves in the sibling texture table. Among the first eight model IDs printed by the Cavern RM2 section inspector, seven resolve to one pair and model `4022622118` resolves to four. This confirms that assuming one texture for every model would lose valid references. The default preview chooses the first resolved pair. The ordered-material preview below provides an explicit experiment for the still-unverified mapping to submodels.

To check whether those material lists could be associated with model submodels, the inspector compares each linked rigid-model material count with the referenced model item's submodel count. The counts match for all 66 linked models in `cavent.rm2` and all 13 in `cavent.sm2`; model `4022622118` is one observed four-material/four-submodel case. `model_inspect` also reports each submodel's source-vertex and candidate-triangle counts, along with its half-open range in the expanded triangle vertex buffer. For `4022622118`, the per-submodel source-vertex/triangle counts are 1,430/843, 425/319, 44/21, and 112/92. This count correspondence supports a possible per-submodel material assignment, but does not prove that list order is the assignment rule. The default preview still uses a single texture; `--candidate-material-order` offers an opt-in visual experiment that assigns the ordered resolved material/texture links to same-index submodels when the counts match. The user reports that this preview looks like Crash's spinning model, providing a visual sanity check for this model item and candidate mapping. That recognition does not confirm the exact material assignment or justify assuming the same rule for every model.

The local game executable contains strings including `.BH`, `.BD`, `%s.mb`, `Crash6\Crash`, `Crash6\Music`, `STREAM.IRX`, `CDVDSTM.IRX`, and `streaming options`.

A direct MIPS reference scan finds `.BH` and `.BD` addresses materialized in adjacent calls to executable address `0x00203ED0` from the routine beginning at `0x002B46A8`. The helper reads the suffix length, updates a caller-provided buffer's length/capacity fields, and invokes copy helpers with the suffix; this supports identifying it as a string-building routine. The caller appends `.BH` to a buffer at its stack base and `.BD` to a second buffer 16 bytes later.

The caller then passes the two resulting buffer pointers in `$a1` and `$a2` to the same routine at `0x002AB688`, with a separate value `5` in `$a3`. That routine forwards its `$a2` argument to `0x002AC1B8` and uses its `$a1` argument as input to another buffer-building call for a field at offset 12 of the object passed in `$a0`. The short helper at `0x002AC1B8` stores the `$a2` buffer pointer at object offset 0 and installs a vtable pointer at offset 4. Thus, both suffix-derived strings are consumed together while initializing a stateful object; `$a3` is used to derive its flags at offset 8. The current static trace does not establish that either string is opened as a filesystem path, nor does it identify the object's later use or the meaning of flag value `5`.

The installed vtable is at `0x00306100`. Its nonzero entries include methods at `0x002AB9F8`, `0x002A8C58`, `0x002ABA78`, `0x002ABAE0`, and `0x002ABC08`. The method at `0x002A8C58` stores a value at object offset 44 and conditionally calls `0x002B6350` with the object's offset-12 string field. That routine lowercases the name, uses the `HOST0:` prefix string while building a lookup key, and calls `0x002B48E0`. The latter searches a count-and-pointer table using 20-byte record strides, builds a candidate key from each probed entry, and calls a string comparison helper against the requested key. Its result is stored in the object's offset-12 field. This supports interpreting the chain as name normalization and table lookup; it does not establish filesystem access or the role of the other stored name.

These call-site observations are consistent with the `.BH` index and `.BD` data being paired, which matches their on-disc lengths and ranges, but do not by themselves prove the exact open/read sequence or all container semantics.

## Next checks

- Determine how the PSX ADPCM-like type-1 music blocks are grouped into streams and channels, and look for structural confirmation beyond header plausibility.
- Inspect the model and mesh payloads in the RM2/SM2 graphics sections and verify their vertex and index layouts against the actual bytes.
- Compare candidate ordered material/submodel assignments across more models and establish whether the apparent correspondence holds outside the initial visual sample.
- Trace how the paired-name object and registry result are consumed after lookup, and determine whether that path reaches file access.
